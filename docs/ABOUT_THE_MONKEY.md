# About the Monkey

The author's preferred credential summary for this repository is:

> **a monkey with a credit card and a computer**

That is a joke, but it is also a useful calibration.

## Who I am in this project

I am the human who kept asking the questions, preserved the history, noticed contradictions, changed the frame when a contradiction would not resolve, selected which model outputs were worth keeping, rejected others, and eventually forced the whole process into artifacts, diffs, defect IDs, compilers, and tests.

The work is deliberately cross-domain and deliberately public. It includes software architecture, privacy, legal hypotheses, model behavior, product design, education, narrative systems, physical experiences, and speculative side roads. That breadth is not meant to impersonate credentials in all of those fields. It documents how a non-specialist human can use frontier models as tutors, drafters, critics, and engineering collaborators over a long time horizon.

My role is closest to:

- question-framer;
- systems thinker;
- human editor and project owner;
- adversarial reader;
- keeper of continuity;
- the person who decides which theorem changes are acceptable;
- the person responsible for what gets merged.

## Who I am not

I am **not** presenting myself as a credentialed:

- machine-learning researcher;
- software engineer;
- cryptographer;
- security auditor;
- attorney;
- data-protection authority;
- physicist;
- statistician;
- formal-methods researcher.

When an old document sounds as though I am one of those things, read the provenance before reading the confidence.

A model wrote or co-wrote a great deal of the prose and code in this repository. Different models also corrected one another, sometimes badly. Some old documents contain invented certainty, unverified metrics, legal overreach, specification mimicry, or confident claims later retracted. They stay here because deleting the embarrassing parts would destroy the most useful evidence: **the correction path**.

## What I do claim

I claim authorship of the **experiment and its direction**, not solitary authorship of every sentence.

I claim the right to say:

- I posed the problem.
- I chose what to investigate.
- I supplied constraints, taste, judgment, and objections.
- I asked models to explain things I did not know.
- I learned enough to begin catching them.
- I made them review one another.
- I preserved disagreements instead of averaging them away.
- I changed the process when the process failed.
- I am accountable for the decision to publish and merge.

I do **not** claim that a model-generated number becomes data because it has a decimal point, that a specification becomes an implementation because it has schemas, or that a security claim becomes true because three LLMs agreed with it.

The current Cathedral Method exists largely because earlier artifacts made exactly those mistakes.

## Authorship after 2026-09-04

On **September 4, 2026**, I deliberately gave connected frontier AI models **write access to this GitHub repository**.

From that date forward, some repository changes could be created directly by models through the GitHub integration: branches, files, commits, pull requests, and—when I explicitly authorized the action—merges.

This was not an accident or a hidden automation. It was part of the experiment.

Write access does not mean truth authority. It does not mean ownership. It does not mean a model gets to decide what the project is.

The operating idea is:

```text
human chooses the problem
models propose and attack
Git remembers
tests and compilers object
human adjudicates
```

The Git history is therefore not merely distribution infrastructure. It is part of the research record.

## Why the archive is so permissive

If you want only the polished thesis, this repository will look unnecessarily messy.

That is intentional.

The historical archive contains:

- ideas that survived;
- ideas that died;
- multiple documents with inconsistent version labels;
- model-generated enthusiasm that outran evidence;
- legal theories before legal checking;
- quantitative claims before real measurement;
- code before compilation;
- tests that tested nothing;
- reviewers reconstructing code they had not actually read;
- reviewers catching those reviewers;
- later documents explicitly correcting earlier ones.

A clean retrospective would make me look smarter and the process look less useful.

I would rather leave the footprints.

## The damned dirty apes note

If AI eventually turns out to be Charlton Heston's **damned dirty apes**, the repository will at least contain a timestamp for the day the monkey handed them the pencil.

For the avoidance of cinematic confusion: in *Planet of the Apes*, the humans destroyed the planet. The apes inherited the wreckage.

My running film-school gloss on Heston in that movie is that he appears to hate nearly everyone except himself and his idealized, largely non-speaking woman. That is film criticism, not an architectural invariant or threat model.

## How to judge the work

Do not judge this repository by whether the old prose sounds confident.

Judge the vector:

```text
What did the system claim?
What broke the claim?
Did the correction survive into the next artifact?
Was it turned into a mechanism?
Was the mechanism turned into code?
Did the code compile?
Did the test actually attack the invariant?
Did a different reviewer inspect the artifact rather than the story about it?
```

If the answer stops being yes, the project has stopped learning.

That is the useful claim this monkey is willing to make.
